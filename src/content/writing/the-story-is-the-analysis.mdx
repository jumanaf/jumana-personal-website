When I started out in techbio, I thought the job was to be right. Clean the data, pick the right model, get a number that holds up. That part matters — but I learned quickly that it's the cheap part.

The expensive part is getting someone to *do* something with the number.

## A model nobody acts on changes nothing

At the bench and in the boardroom, the same thing kept happening: a beautiful analysis would land, everyone would nod, and then nothing would change. Not because the work was wrong — because it never became a decision.

A chart is not an argument. A p-value is not a plan. The gap between "here is what the data says" and "here is what we should do" is where most of the value either appears or evaporates.

## What storytelling actually means

I don't mean dressing things up. I mean:

- **Start from the decision, not the dataset.** What will change depending on the answer? If nothing, the analysis is a hobby.
- **Say the thing.** Lead with the conclusion, then show the evidence. Make people work to disagree, not to understand.
- **Respect the audience's model of the world.** A lab scientist, a software engineer, and an exec are three different readers. The data is the same; the story isn't.

> The best analysis I ever did wasn't the most sophisticated. It was the one that fit on one slide and made the next step obvious.

## Why I care about the craft

Working across a scaling startup — from ~50 people to ~200 — I sat between pure engineers and pure biologists, and the translation *was* the job. The insight only counted once it moved someone.

So now I treat the story as part of the analysis, not a wrapper around it. If I can't explain why it matters and what to do next, I'm not done yet.
